# Information theory, optimal measurement and experimental design

**General research engineering textbook.** Applies to independent experiments in optics, quantum sensors, microwave devices and other Rydberg platforms.

## Competencies

Define observables, quantify uncertainties and physical scaling, select an appropriate model, and apply controlled numerical and laboratory validation.

## 1. What is learned from an experiment

An experiment estimates parameters θ from data y generated by a forward model. Information content depends on how strongly the expected signal changes when θ changes relative to noise, not on the number of colorful data points. If observations are independent Gaussian values with sensitivity row a_i and variance σ_i², the Fisher contribution is a_iᵀa_i/σ_i². For a vector θ, sum the contributions to obtain a matrix whose eigenvectors reveal strong and weak parameter directions.

## 2. Deriving the Fisher information matrix

Let y=Aθ+ε and ε∼N(0,C), with known parameter-independent covariance C. The negative log likelihood is 1/2(y−Aθ)ᵀC⁻¹(y−Aθ) plus a constant. Differentiation and expectation lead to F=AᵀC⁻¹A. When C depends on θ, additional derivative terms arise; using the simpler formula unchanged can be incorrect. The covariance lower bound of an unbiased regular estimator is related to F⁻¹ under the required regularity assumptions.

## 3. Time allocation and signal-dependent noise

If measurement state i is acquired for t_i and independent noise variance scales as σ_i²/t_i, then F=∑(t_i/σ_i²)a_iᵀa_i. A fixed total time budget ∑t_i=T creates a design optimization problem. An A-optimal objective may minimize Tr(F⁻¹), D-optimality may maximize log det F, and E-optimality may improve the weakest mode. These criteria reflect different scientific goals. If the readout has laser drift or technical noise floors, 1/t variance scaling may break down.

## 4. Information geometry and numerical stability

The singular spectrum of a whitened sensitivity matrix C^−1/2 A determines local observability. Row normalisation can clarify geometry but remove absolute signal strength, while a gain-aware metric retains physical detector sensitivity. A design with attractive normalized condition number may produce negligible volts at the detector. Report both state diversity and signal/noise calibration, and define all field or parameter units before comparing experiments.

## 5. Sequential and adaptive experiments

Bayesian or frequentist adaptive design chooses future settings based on previous observations. This can focus time on uncertain directions, but if experimental drift is unmodeled it may chase a moving target. A robust protocol maintains independent reference states interleaved with measurement states and records settings chosen by the algorithm. A stopping rule should depend on uncertainty or predictive validation, not on obtaining one preferred value.

## 6. Case study: two-dimensional parameter sensing

Suppose A has rows (1,0),(0,0.1) and identical unit measurement noise. Then F is diagonal (1,0.01) and model covariance is diagonal (1,100): the second parameter is poorly observed. Adding another row (0,1) changes F to diagonal (1,1.01), reducing its second variance to about 0.990. Adding hundreds of copies of the first row would barely help the second parameter. This shows why independence and physical gain are central.

## 7. Verification and reporting

Publish the full sensitivity matrix, state order, covariance, time budget, objective and optimizer bounds. Test analytic two-parameter limits, re-run with alternative noise models, and report actual holdout estimator errors. An optimization that improves a calculated inverse-trace objective is not automatically a measured improvement in sensitivity or experimental receiver performance.

## End-of-chapter questions with explanations

### Question 1. Why do repeated measurements along one mode fail to measure another?

<details><summary>Reveal answer and reasoning</summary>

They contribute information primarily along the original sensitivity direction, leaving orthogonal parameter combinations weak.

</details>

### Question 2. When is F=AᵀC⁻¹A valid?

<details><summary>Reveal answer and reasoning</summary>

For the specified linear or local Gaussian model with appropriate covariance assumptions.

</details>

### Question 3. What distinguishes A-, D- and E-optimality?

<details><summary>Reveal answer and reasoning</summary>

They optimise trace of inverse information, log determinant and weakest information direction, respectively.

</details>

### Question 4. Why can row normalization mislead hardware design?

<details><summary>Reveal answer and reasoning</summary>

It can hide how little absolute signal a state produces relative to physical noise.

</details>

### Question 5. What is needed before claiming sensitivity improvement?

<details><summary>Reveal answer and reasoning</summary>

Independent calibration of gain/noise plus experimental or held-out estimator error, not only an optimized mathematical objective.

</details>

### Question 6. Why can adaptive designs be biased by drift?

<details><summary>Reveal answer and reasoning</summary>

A changing calibration may be misidentified as informative parameter variation.

</details>

## Practice and reproducibility challenge

Create a new model or experiment with declared units, physical response and calibration reference. Test its weakest identifiable mode, document uncertainty assumptions and keep at least one control or holdout dataset independent from optimisation. Archive failed cases with the same provenance as accepted cases.
